Before You Start...

The PAR Method

Structure behavioral answers around three parts. This tool checks your answers for all three, plus role-specific technical terms.

P — PROBLEM

The situation or challenge you faced. Set the scene in about 15–20 seconds — just enough for the interviewer to get the stakes.

A — ACTION

What you specifically did about it. Be concrete — name the tools, decisions, and steps. Say "I," not "we."

R — RESULT

The outcome, ideally with a number — time saved, errors reduced, users served. No number? Describe the outcome concretely instead.

Example — even a technical question can use PAR

"How would you approach optimizing a slow-running SQL query?"

"Our reporting dashboard's main query was timing out during peak hours (Problem). I ran EXPLAIN on the query, found it was doing a full table scan because a join column wasn't indexed, and added the missing index along with a covering index for the most common filter (Action). Query time dropped from 12 seconds to under 200ms, and the dashboard stopped timing out (Result)."

No Full-Time Experience? Answer Anyway

Entry-level interviewers evaluate your thinking process, not your resume depth. Substitute real experience with these five pivots.

  • Pivot to academic & group projects — a capstone design disagreement demonstrates the same conflict-resolution skill as a workplace one.
  • Highlight personal projects — a portfolio scraper, dashboard, or game shows self-motivation and real problem-solving.
  • Use the hypothetical pivot — "I haven't hit that professionally yet, but here's the exact framework I'd use."
  • Draw from non-tech jobs — de-escalating a frustrated customer transfers directly to IT support or stakeholder management.
  • Focus on the how, not the what — narrate your specific troubleshooting steps, not just the final answer.
